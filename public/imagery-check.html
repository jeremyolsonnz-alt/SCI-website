Interactive · Self-Reflection Tool

How Do You Imagine?

People imagine in very different ways. Some see pictures. Some feel movement without seeing anything at all. This short tool helps you find your own way in — so the guided imagery practices fit how your mind actually works, instead of how someone guessed it would.

Start here — this is the important part

Imagining movement doesn't need feeling to work. It happens in your brain, in the parts that plan movement — the same parts that planned every move you have ever made. It doesn't depend on signals coming back up from your body.

That's why guided imagery can work after a spinal cord injury — whatever your level, whatever you can feel. The injury blocked the road. It didn't knock down the city the road came from.

What comes next is not a test of how much movement you have. It asks how your imagination works. That's a completely different thing.

Why This Matters

Guided imagery works by switching on the same brain networks that real movement uses. The clearer you can imagine, the more strongly those networks switch on — and the more the practice can do. Clear imagining is the engine of the whole programme.

But people imagine in very different ways, and most programmes never check. They hand you a script that says picture your hand closing. Someone who can't picture anything decides they're doing it wrong, and stops. They weren't doing it wrong. They were just given the wrong instructions.

There are two ways to imagine. You can be strong at one and weak at the other.

Way One
Seeing

Seeing the movement in your mind, like watching it: the shape of the hand, where the arm is, the room around you. Most people think this is what "imagining" means. For a lot of people, it isn't their stronger side at all.

Way Two
Feeling

Feeling the movement from the inside, without seeing anything: the weight, the effort, the stretch, the warmth in your palm, the sense of where your arm is. For getting movement back, this is the more important side — and it's the one this tool cares about most.

This is a feeling your brain makes on its own. It isn't the same as feeling that travels up from your body, and it doesn't need it.

Part One · Warming Up Your Imagination

Five minutes · Do this first

You can get better at imagining. It gets clearer the more you do it, like any skill. This warm-up starts with easy, everyday things and works up to movement — because starting with imagine your hand closing is starting with the hardest thing of all.

Give each one twenty or thirty seconds. Nothing is scored here. This just wakes your imagination up.

Warm-up One · An object
A lemon

Picture a lemon on a plate in front of you. Its yellow. The bumpy, waxy skin. Now imagine picking it up — the weight, the cool surface, the slight give. Now imagine cutting it, and the sharp smell rising up.

Notice which parts were easy. The colour? The weight? The smell? That already tells you something about how you imagine.

Warm-up Two · A place
A room you know well

Think of a room you know really well. Don't try to see all of it at once. Start at the door. Move slowly. Where is the light coming from? What sounds belong in this room?

If pictures don't come, notice what does. Lots of people get a strong sense of a place — how it's laid out, how it feels — without seeing anything. That sense is real imagining, and you can use it.

Warm-up Three · A movement
Something you can still do

Now something with movement in it — and on purpose, a movement you can still do. Turning your head to look over your shoulder, maybe. Or a shrug. Or taking a slow, deep breath.

Do it for real first. Then close your eyes and imagine doing it. Not watching yourself — doing it. Feel the turn, the stretch, the effort, the settling back.

Take your time — as long as the real movement takes. Rushing is the most common mistake, and it makes imagining much weaker.

Part Two · The Self-Check

Four minutes

This check does one slightly unusual thing, and it's the whole point. It asks you to rate your imagining twice: first for a movement you can still do, then for a movement your injury affects.

The first set shows your starting point — how clearly you imagine in general. The second shows how clearly you imagine the affected part. The difference between them is the useful bit, and a normal imagery quiz won't show you that.

Rate each one honestly. There are no good or bad scores — only useful information. Nothing you type is saved, sent, or seen by anyone. Your result shows up on this page and disappears when you close it.

Part A · Your starting point — a movement you can still do

Do each movement for real first, if you can. Then close your eyes and imagine it. Then rate the imagining — not the movement.

1. Imagine turning your head to look over your shoulder. How clearly did you SEE it?
2. The same head turn. How strongly did you FEEL it — the turn, the stretch in your neck?
3. Imagine taking a slow, deep breath on purpose. How clearly did you SEE it — your chest rising, your shoulders lifting?
4. The same deep breath. How strongly did you FEEL it — the fullness, then letting go?
Part B · The movement you picked

Now imagine the movement you picked above. Don't try to judge whether it "worked" or whether anything moved. Just rate how clear the imagining was.

5. Imagine closing your hand around an object. How clearly did you SEE it?
6. The same movement. How strongly did you FEEL it from the inside — the effort, the weight, where it is?
7. Imagine the same movement again, slower, in real time. How clearly did you SEE it this time?
8. That slower version. How strongly did you FEEL it?
Please answer all eight before carrying on.

What to do in the practices

    Three Things That Are True, Whatever Your Score

    One
    This is not a measure of how much movement you have

    This is the most important line on the page. A faint feeling-image of your hand does not mean there's nothing there. A strong one does not mean there is. They're separate things. How clear an image is tells you about your imagination, not about your spinal cord.

    Please don't let a low score here become a verdict about your body. It isn't one, and it can't be one.

    Two
    Imagining gets stronger with practice

    Imagining gets clearer the more you do it. Someone who starts with faint, shaky images and practises regularly for a few weeks will usually imagine much more clearly than when they began. The warm-up above is the training. Use it before every session, and take this check again in three weeks.

    Three
    Watch, then imagine, then try

    Watching a real movement switches on many of the same brain areas as doing it. It also makes the imagining that comes right after much stronger. So before you imagine, watch: a video, a family member's hand opening and closing, your own working movement in a mirror. Then imagine it. Then try it — no matter what happens, because trying makes a stronger signal than imagining alone.

    Watch → imagine → try. That order beats imagining on its own for almost everyone. And if your imagining is faint, it's a lifeline.

    An honest note about this tool

    This is a tool to help you reflect, not a medical test. It hasn't been formally tested, and you shouldn't use it to make decisions about your care.

    It's built on ideas from proper imagery questionnaires — keeping seeing and feeling separate, doing the movement before imagining it, and rating how clear it was out of five. But it isn't one of those official questionnaires, and it doesn't copy one. The official one is called the KVIQ (the Kinesthetic and Visual Imagery Questionnaire), made by Malouin and her team. If you want a formal check, ask your therapist to do the KVIQ with you.

    The point here is simpler: to help you notice how you imagine, before you start, so the practices fit your actual mind instead of a guessed one. Whatever your result, it changes how you use the practices — never whether you can.

    Why Check How You Imagine At All?

    Imagining movement isn't one fixed ability that everyone has in equal amounts. It varies a lot from person to person, and that seems to matter: people who imagine more clearly tend to get more out of mental practice. This is one of the most overlooked points in the whole field. Handing everyone the same script and assuming it works the same way for all of them is a mistake.

    Even so, how well someone imagines is rarely checked before a mental practice programme starts. So people who imagine faintly, or who can feel movement but can't see it, get instructions built for someone else's mind, decide they're failing, and drop out.

    The Two Ways of Imagining

    Seeing a movement and feeling a movement are two different abilities, and they can come apart — you can be strong at one and weak at the other. This split is built into the main tool used in rehab, the Kinesthetic and Visual Imagery Questionnaire (KVIQ), made and tested by Malouin and her team for people with stroke and other conditions.

    For getting movement back, the feeling side seems to matter more. One well-known idea, from Jeannerod, is that imagining a movement is really your brain quietly running the movement command itself — not making a picture of it. If that's right, then the feeling of effort, force, and position is closer to what mental practice is trying to reach than the picture is.

    Imagining Movement After Spinal Cord Injury

    The idea behind the whole programme is that imagining movement happens in the brain, from the top down, and doesn't need signals coming back up from the body. The injury blocks the movement command from getting through. It doesn't remove the brain's ability to make one.

    Brain scans have shown, again and again, that people with spinal cord injury can still switch on their movement networks when they imagine movements they can no longer do. This is the same finding that makes brain–computer interface research possible: the movement part of the brain is still producing signals you can use.

    There is a catch, though, and this tool is built around it. The brain's "map" of the body isn't fixed — it changes when the signals coming in change, and this map shifts after a spinal cord injury. Some studies have found that imagining movements below the injury can be slower or less clear than imagining movements that still work, and that this may depend on how long ago the injury happened. The findings aren't all the same, and there's less research here than for stroke.

    This is why the check asks about a movement you can still do and a movement your injury affects. Someone whose imagining is faint everywhere is in a different spot from someone who imagines clearly in general but has gone faint for the affected part — and they need different advice. A normal questionnaire won't tell those two apart.

    Aphantasia

    A small number of people can't make pictures in their mind at all, on purpose — a lifelong thing called aphantasia, described in recent research by Zeman and his team. It isn't caused by injury, it isn't a fault to fix, and it's common enough that an imagery programme shouldn't ignore it.

    Importantly, aphantasia is usually about seeing only. Many people with aphantasia can still feel movement in their mind, and can imagine movement that way. Any programme that treats picturing as the only door leaves these people out for no reason.

    Imagining Can Be Trained

    How clearly you imagine isn't set in stone. It responds to practice. A common way to build it is to start with simple, everyday images that use several senses, and work up to the movement you're aiming for. That's the reason for the three warm-ups: starting someone with faint imagery on "imagine your hand closing" is starting at the hardest point.

    The Limits of This Tool

    Read this before you draw any conclusions

    This tool hasn't been tested. It's an original reflection tool built on solid ideas, but it hasn't been checked for reliability, it doesn't copy the KVIQ, and its score cut-offs are sensible guesses, not proven numbers.

    There's less research on imagining after SCI than after stroke. A lot of the reasoning above is carried over from the wider research on imagining movement, and from stroke studies. Where SCI findings exist, they don't all agree.

    How you imagine does not predict recovery. Nothing here is a sign of how your recovery will go. The link between clear imagining and getting more from mental practice is about training — not about the state of your spinal cord.

    Rating yourself has limits. People aren't perfect judges of their own imagining, and your ratings can be swayed by mood, tiredness, and what you expect. Treat the result as a starting point for a conversation, not a measurement.
